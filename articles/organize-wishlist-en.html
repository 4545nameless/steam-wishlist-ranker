How to Organize a Steam Wishlist That Got Too Big

You add every game that looks interesting, and one day your wishlist has 100, then 200 games. If you use Steam, this probably sounds familiar. This article covers what goes wrong with an oversized wishlist and a low-effort way to bring it back under control.

What goes wrong when a wishlist gets too big

You can't decide what to buy during a sale

When a big sale starts, discount badges show up all over your wishlist. Everything looks like a deal, so you scroll from top to bottom and end up buying nothing, or you buy a pile on impulse and never get around to playing them. The more games there are, the worse this gets.

The games you really wanted get buried

Left alone, a wishlist sits in the order you added things. The game you've wanted for years sinks to the bottom while something you added on a whim last week sits near the top.

Too many notifications, so you stop reading them

Steam tells you when wishlisted games go on sale or launch. With hundreds of games, notifications arrive almost every day, and soon you skim past the ones that matter.

Idea 1: don't compare everything

The main reason people give up on reordering is the feeling that every game needs an exact position. Comparing 200 games head to head means about 20,000 pairs. Nobody can do that by hand.

But for actual shopping, only the top of the list matters. In the next sale you'll buy a handful of games, maybe a dozen. Whether a game is #100 or #101 makes almost no difference. Be careful at the top and rough everywhere else. That's what makes organizing sustainable.

Idea 2: sort into tiers

Instead of exact positions, split games into a few groups. Decisions become much easier. Four tiers work well:

TierRough sizeWhat to do in a sale
Want it nowAbout 10%Buy at a decent discount
Buy on saleAbout 20%Buy when the price drops a lot
Maybe somedayThe largest groupSkip for now
Probably skipThe restCandidates to remove

It's normal for "Maybe someday" to be the biggest tier. You don't need to agonize over those games every sale. Narrowing your attention to the top two tiers changes how you shop.

Idea 3: decide what you don't want, too

Finding games you no longer want matters as much as picking favorites. Plenty of games seemed interesting when you added them but leave you cold now.

Asking "should I remove this?" one game at a time is tiring, though. Put a few games side by side and ask "which of these do I want least?" and the answer comes quickly. Having something to compare against makes your feelings clear.

Idea 4: review before big sales

Tastes change. Rather than ordering once and forgetting it, review before each big sale. Where do your new additions fit? Are your top games still the same? You don't have to start from scratch; slot in the new games and check the top.
